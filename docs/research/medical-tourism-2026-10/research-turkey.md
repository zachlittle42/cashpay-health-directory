# Turkey Medical Tourism: Research Report for an American Cash-Pay Audience

Research date: 2026-10-30. Sources favored: Turkish Ministry of Health (MoH), USHAŞ, TÜİK/TurkStat-derived figures, Official Gazette, UK FCDO, BAAPS, BDA, CDC, ISHRS, peer-reviewed journals, and major news outlets. Clinic and broker pages are used only for price ranges and are flagged as **marketing** wherever cited. Every figure carries a numbered source; "unsourced" items are omitted or listed in the gaps section.

---

## 1. Market overview

**Official volumes and revenue.** USHAŞ (International Health Services Inc., the MoH's state health-tourism company) publishes an annual series that matches the TÜİK tourism survey. Health visitors and revenue (USD thousands) [1][2]:

| Year | Health visitors | Revenue (USD thousands) |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 756,926 | 1,459,132 |
| 2020 | 435,691 | 1,371,189 |
| 2021 | 729,592 | 2,016,262 |
| 2022 | 1,381,807 | 2,206,750 |
| 2023 | 1,538,643 | 3,006,092 |
| 2024 | 1,506,442 | 3,022,957 |
| 2025 | 1,398,580 | 3,022,452 |

Rows 2019–2024 are directly from the USHAŞ data page [1]. The 2025 row is from a Turkish compilation citing TÜİK [2] and was repeated by a TÜRSAB (travel agencies association) senior advisor in October 2026 ("about 1.4 million patients, about $3 billion, roughly $2,200 per patient") [3]. I could not open the TÜİK bulletin itself; treat 2025 as "official-derived, secondary-reported."

Key reading: visitor volume fell about 7% in 2025 while revenue held flat, so revenue per patient rose. First-half 2026 continued that pattern: 563,327 health visitors (below H1 2025) and about $1.58 billion, roughly $2,600 per patient [3].

**Government targets vs reality.** Official ambitions are far above these outturns. Industry and ministry-adjacent figures talked of 2 million patients and $10–12 billion for 2025 and $20 billion by 2028 [4][5]. The $12 billion figure appears to use a broader definition than the TÜİK series; no source reconciles the two. NPR cited "about 2 million medical tourists in 2024, close to $3 billion" from the Health Ministry [6], which again conflicts with the 1.5 million USHAŞ count for the same year. Use the USHAŞ/TÜİK series and say so.

**Source countries.** No official country table was found. A Medicana executive quoted by Anadolu Agency (Feb 2025) named Germany, the UK, Russia, Iraq, Azerbaijan and Middle Eastern countries as the main senders [4]. The US does not appear in any official top-sender list located. A 2009 Investment Office item claimed US patients had "surpassed Europeans" that year [7], but it is 17 years old and unverified. The only 2025 US-specific datapoint is a clinic claim via NPR that about a third of Smile Hair Clinic's roughly 5,000 annual patients are American [6] (**marketing**, single clinic). Americans in Turkey: unsourced as a national count.

**Procedures.** Officials and industry consistently name aesthetic surgery, dental, eye, hair transplant, bariatric surgery, IVF, oncology and transplant as the core lines [4][5]. The Turkish Health Tourism Association told NPR about 1 million people came for hair transplants in 2022, spending about $2 billion [6]. A dermatologist at Nişantaşı Hospital said in 2019 that about 5,000 hair transplants a day are performed in Istanbul and that the sector earned over $1 billion in 2018 (DEİK data) [8]. These are association or industry figures, not TÜİK.

**Cities.** No official city split was found. TÜRSAB's advisor described Antalya as the second-largest health tourism market after Istanbul, with foreigners making up about 90% of patients at Antalya dental clinics [3]. Istanbul's clinic cluster sits on the European side around Şişli, Nişantaşı, Fulya, Mecidiyeköy and Levent [9]. Izmir and Ankara volumes: unsourced.

**Authorized facilities.** The MoH listed 654 hospitals, 189 medical centers, 2,525 clinics and 1,299 other facilities holding health tourism authorization as of 24 July 2024 [10].

## 2. Procedures Americans travel for, with prices

US figures below are surgeon-fee averages from the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS) cost pages, which exclude anesthesia, facility and related fees [11]. Turkish figures are package quotes from clinics and brokers (**marketing**) and are typically all-inclusive (surgery, hospital night(s), hotel, transfers). The two are not like-for-like; a US all-in bill is usually 1.5–2x the surgeon fee.

### Hair transplant
- Turkey packages: most sources cluster at $2,500–$4,500 all-inclusive (hotel, transfers, meds), roughly $1–2 per graft; a few run to $10,000+ for named-surgeon clinics [12][13] (**marketing**). An American nurse profiled by NPR paid about $3,400 including airfare and lodging [6].
- US: Bosley says most procedures run $6,000–$12,000 (one section says $4,400–$12,000) [14]. NPR cited a 2023 ASPS figure "upwards of $13,000" [6].
- Graft counts: the ISHRS 2025 Practice Census (2024 data, 946 valid invitations, ±5.4% margin) found the average first-procedure session was 2,347 grafts and a subsequent session 1,637; typical FUE case averaged 2,262 grafts [15]. ISHRS flags "4,500 grafts in a single session" as a red flag for an exhausted donor area [16].
- Techniques marketed as FUE, "sapphire FUE" (sapphire blade for channels) and DHI (implanter pen). No regulator or peer-reviewed source located ranks them; treat technique names as marketing differentiators.

### Dental
- Veneers: Turkey $145–$400 per tooth (PlacidWay June 2026 dataset, average about $247) vs US $900–$2,500 per tooth [17][18] (**marketing** both sides).
- Single implant: Turkey $250–$950 depending on brand [19]; US complete implant with crown about $4,500 national average, range $3,000–$6,000 [20].
- All-on-4: Turkey $2,000–$6,000 per arch; US $15,000–$30,000+ per arch [21] (**marketing**).
- "Turkey teeth": UK slang for aggressive full-coverage crowns on healthy teeth, popularized by reality-TV figures; a Love Island remark drove a reported 10,000% search spike in June 2023 [22]. The BDA's 2022 survey of UK dentists: 94% had seen patients treated abroad, 86% had treated resulting problems; crowns (87%) and implants (85%) were the treatments most often needing remedial work; 65% of repairs cost at least £500 and one in five exceeded £5,000; over 40% of remedial work fell on the NHS [23][24]. The core issue dentists describe is patients booking "veneers" and receiving full crowns on filed-down teeth [23][25].

### Cosmetic surgery (US = ASPS average surgeon fee only [11])
| Procedure | US ASPS surgeon fee | Turkey package (marketing) |
|---|---|---|
| Rhinoplasty | $7,637 | $4,000–$15,000 [26] |
| Tummy tuck | $8,174 | £2,900–£4,200 all-inclusive [27] |
| BBL (fat grafting) | $7,264 | not reliably sourced |
| Breast augmentation | $4,875 (implants) | $2,800–$5,500 [28] |
| Facelift | $11,395 | $3,000–$10,000 [29] |
| Liposuction | $4,711 | not reliably sourced |

Mommy makeover: no reliable Turkish range located; UK cases involving combined tummy tuck plus breast and liposuction packages are documented in the safety section.

### Bariatric
- Turkey gastric sleeve: $3,200–$7,500 all-inclusive (Acıbadem's own 2026 guide gives $3,500–$7,500; brokers $3,200–$4,500) [30][31] (**marketing**). UK press documented prices "as little as £2,000" [32].
- US self-pay sleeve: national average about $14,900, range $9,350–$24,000 [33].
- Bypass and balloon: no reliable Turkey vs US pair located.

### Eye
- LASIK: US average about $4,492 for both eyes (American Refractive Surgery Council figure, cited secondhand) vs Turkey $1,000–$2,500 per eye or €1,200–€2,800 all-inclusive [34][35] (**marketing**). SMILE in Turkey €2,000–€3,400 both eyes; ICL €2,800–€6,500; cataract monofocal $1,200–$2,000 per eye, with premium lenses €3,500–€7,500 that narrow the saving [35].

### IVF and fertility
- Legal frame: Turkish law permits assisted reproduction only for legally married heterosexual couples using their own gametes. Egg, sperm and embryo donation and surrogacy are prohibited; gender selection is restricted to sex-linked disease [36][37]. A 2010 amendment made receiving donor gametes abroad a criminal matter for Turkish citizens (up to three years), and clinics referring patients abroad face closure [38]. These rules apply to foreign patients treated in Turkey.
- Cost: $2,500–$5,500 per cycle, medications usually extra, ICSI $500–$1,200, with 15–20 days on site for a fresh cycle [36][37] (**marketing**).

### Orthopedics
- Hip replacement: Turkey €9,100–€16,900 (Acıbadem Q3 2026 index) or $7,500–$12,000 (brokers); US $30,000–$50,000 [39][40]. Knee: Turkey $7,000–$12,800; US $35,000–$70,000 [40][41] (**marketing**).

### Cardiac and oncology
- CABG: Turkey $10,000–$20,000 vs US $57,000–$150,000+ [42] (**marketing**). No independent outcome data by hospital was found. Oncology package pricing: unsourced.

### Health check-ups
- Acıbadem lists check-ups at $450–$1,200 on its German-language page; third-party guides put executive packages at $1,200–$1,800 [43][44] (**marketing**).

## 3. How the system works

**Legal basis.** The 2017 "Regulation on International Health Tourism and Tourist Health" (Official Gazette 30123, 13 July 2017) created the International Health Tourism Authorization Certificate. Facilities obtain it from the MoH; intermediary agencies obtain theirs from USHAŞ; uncertified operators can be shut down [45]. A new regulation of the same name replaced it on 26 April 2025 (Official Gazette 32882) and repealed the 2017 text [46][47].

**What the 2025 regulation adds** [46][47][48]:
- Complication insurance mandatory for surgical and interventional procedures done in an operating room, deadline end of 2025 (one law-firm summary says enforced from 1 January 2026).
- TÜSKA (Türkiye Health Care Quality and Accreditation Institute) accreditation mandatory for hospitals, medical centers, labs and dialysis centers; other facilities need an MoH certificate; deadline end of 2026 [49].
- Mandatory registration and tracking on the HealthTürkiye portal; a named responsible staff member per patient; at least one certified foreign-language speaker; 24/7 call service in two languages for intermediaries; foreign-language website.
- Certificates are non-transferable and branch-specific; USHAŞ evaluates intermediaries at least annually, with 3- and 6-month suspensions and cancellation for failing re-evaluation.
- Facilities and intermediaries assessed on complaint rates and satisfaction surveys.

**How a consumer verifies a certificate.** The MoH Health Tourism Department site (shgmturizmdb.saglik.gov.tr) links the current lists of authorized facilities and authorized intermediary organizations; the facility list was updated 5 October 2026 [50]. The FCDO points British patients to the MoH's HealthTürkiye portal (healthturkiye.gov.tr) for approved providers, while stating it does not endorse any practitioner [51][52]. Practical step: ask the clinic for its certificate number and expiry, then find it on the MoH list. The certificate confirms authorization to treat foreigners; it is not a quality rating [48].

**JCI.** Independent counts put Turkey at about 38 hospitals accredited under JCI's hospital/academic programs, or about 42 organizations of all types, as of August 2026; older marketing claims of 47–60 are out of date [53][54]. Named JCI holders include Anadolu Medical Center [55] and Medipol Mega University Hospital (first in Turkey accredited as an Academic Medical Center) [56]; Acıbadem reports 7 JCI-accredited hospitals [57]; Florence Nightingale reports JCI since 2004 [58]. Verify any specific campus in JCI's directory.

**Hair transplants: hospital vs clinic vs technician.** The Regulation on Hair Transplant Units (Official Gazette 32182, 6 May 2023) allows units only inside licensed hospitals, medical centers and polyclinics, requires Ministry-registered certificates for practitioners, requires every procedure to be logged in the MoH electronic system, and requires facilities to be listed on the Ministry website [59][60]. Who does what: opening the channels (incisions) is physician-only (certified physician, plastic surgeon or dermatologist); harvesting follicles and placing grafts may be done by certified non-physician health staff under physician supervision [61]. A September 2025 amendment extended the transitional compliance deadline to 31 December 2026, grandfathered pre-2023 authorized facilities, and set a new inspection form [62].

**The controversy.** The MoH's own Director General of Health Services said in 2018 that in Turkey a physician typically performs about the first two hours of an eight-hour procedure and hands the rest to support staff, whereas abroad plastic surgeons do the whole operation, "which makes it more expensive" [63]. In October 2025, plastic surgeon Prof. Derya Özçelik, dermatologist Dr. Murat Küçüktaş and the president of the Legal Physicians Institute Association criticized the split as a patient-safety risk and as handing legally reserved medical authority (Law 1219) to non-physicians [61]. The Turkish Health Tourism Association president said in 2021 that "about 50% of Istanbul hair transplant centers sometimes operate without a doctor," framed as hearsay, not data [64]. A Turkish Medical Association (TTB) statement specifically on hair transplants was not located; the Turkish Society of Plastic Reconstructive and Aesthetic Surgeons and ISHRS have both issued warnings about illegal clinics [65]. ISHRS 2025 census: 59.4% of members report black-market clinics in their city; on average 10% of members' cases were repairs of prior black-market transplants [15]. Scale estimates of "six in ten Istanbul clinics operating illegally" trace to 2016 reporting and are unverified today [65].

## 4. Safety and consumer protection

**Deaths.** The FCDO's Turkey health page states: "We are aware of 7 British nationals having died in Turkey in 2025 following medical procedures," and that others needed further treatment or surgery [51]. Earlier FCDO counts: 17 (mid-2022), 22 (Dec 2022), 24, then 28 British deaths after elective procedures in Turkey between March 2019 and March 2024, per a Foreign Office minister's answer to Parliament [66][67]. One secondary site rendered the 2025 figure as "79"; the live gov.uk text says 7 [51][68].

Documented cases:
- Bariatric: an Independent investigation found at least seven British deaths after weight-loss surgery in Turkey since 2019 (Mar 2023) [32]. Inquests: Leanne Leary, 38, pulmonary embolism after sleeve surgery (2023) [69]; Janet Savage, 54, aortic injury during sleeve surgery, 5 Aug 2023, booked via a UK travel firm [70]; Morgan Ribeiro, 20, peritonitis from a ruptured bowel [71].
- BBL: Kaydell Brown, 38, died at Clinic Expert in Istanbul in Aug 2024; the clinic cited fat embolism and said its internal review found no malpractice [72]. Hayley Dowell, 38, died Oct 2023 after a BBL, tummy tuck and liposuction costing over £7,000; her husband told the coroner the surgeon left midway [73]. Earlier BBL deaths: Melissa Kerr (2019), Leah Cambridge (2018) [72].
- Hair transplant: Martyn Latchman, 38, from Milton Keynes, died 28 July 2025 after falling ill at a Beşiktaş clinic; Turkish authorities opened an investigation and BAAPS issued a statement [74][75]. Mentor Rama, 36, died in Istanbul in Nov 2025 after a hair transplant and a next-day dental appointment; autopsy pending at time of reporting [76].
- Other nationals: a Canadian woman, 35, died 7 March 2026 after a tummy tuck and breast lift at a private hospital in Antalya's Kepez district; a criminal investigation was opened [68].

**Complication data (UK, the best-documented source population).**
- BAAPS database study (Sept 2022–Sept 2024, 198 patients): 76% of complications followed surgery in Turkey; nearly half needed further surgery under general anesthetic; one death from pulmonary embolism; NHS cost up to £1.8 million [77].
- BAAPS Nov 2023 update: UK hospital admissions after cosmetic surgery abroad rose 94% in three years; Turkey accounted for 78% of corrective operations since Nov 2022; BAAPS's working estimate was about £15,000 per patient [78].
- BAAPS Nov 2024 (103 referred patients): 83% chose abroad for cost; 57% paid under £5,000 all-in; 66% would not do it again; wound problems 33% and seroma 29% of complications [79].
- BMJ Open rapid review (Feb 2026; 37 studies, 655 NHS-treated patients 2006–2024): Turkey was the destination in 61% of cases; 385 patients were bariatric and 265 cosmetic; up to 53% had moderate to severe complications; NHS cost £1,058–£19,549 per patient at 2024 prices [80].
- Dental: BDA 2022 survey figures above [23][24]. UK case reports in 2025 include a patient left toothless after implants failed within three months of a £5,000 procedure [81] and a man who developed a brain infection from a sinus infection after dental work in Turkey [82].

**Antibiotic-resistant infections.** A 2023 Journal of Travel Medicine case report describes a carbapenem-resistant ST773 Pseudomonas aeruginosa wound infection after cosmetic surgery in Turkey [83]. BAAPS noted in 2023 that some returning patients carry multi-resistant bacteria [78]. CDC's medical tourism page lists antimicrobial-resistant outbreaks among medical tourists as a known risk and recommends delaying air travel 10–14 days after major surgery because of clot risk [84]. CDC's documented outbreak investigation (38 US bariatric patients with VIM-producing carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas, 2018–2019) concerned Tijuana, not Turkey [85]. No CDC or ECDC Turkey-specific alert was found.

**Legal recourse.** Turkish law-firm guidance says foreign patients can file MoH complaints and civil claims, can proceed via power of attorney without staying in Turkey, and are entitled to their records; obstacles are that proceedings and records are in Turkish, and claims typically arise after the patient has left [86]. These are law-firm marketing pages; no statute of limitations or case outcome data was found. Several UK inquests were delayed because the Turkish surgeon could not be traced or no cause of death was supplied [71][72]. The 2025 regulation's complication insurance requirement is new and its claims process for foreigners is untested in any source found [46][47]. Package "guarantees" (free revision, "lifetime warranty") are contractual promises by the clinic; no source describes an enforcement mechanism beyond Turkish courts.

**Advertising and enforcement.** The UK Advertising Standards Authority banned several Turkish clinics' ads in 2023 for trivializing surgery by selling it as a "holiday" or "VIP package" [87][88]. The UK health secretary pledged a crackdown on cosmetic surgery "cowboys" abroad in Nov 2024 [89]. In Turkey, the MoH pledged stricter oversight and more inspections (NPR, Oct 2025) while critics cite an estimated 5,000 clinics in Istanbul [6]; a specific 2025–2026 raid campaign or closure list was not found.

## 5. Practical logistics for Americans

- **Entry:** Turkey's Foreign Ministry lists US ordinary passport holders as visa-exempt for up to 90 days in any 180-day period [90]; the e-visa and its $52 fee were dropped from 1 January (year not shown on the page; travel press reported the change) [91]. The State Department rates Türkiye Level 2, "exercise increased caution," for terrorism and arbitrary detention, and has no medical-tourism-specific warning [92].
- **Flights:** Turkish Airlines nonstops: New York about 10 hours out, 11–12 back [93]; Chicago about 10h30–10h40 [94]; Los Angeles about 13h [95]; Denver about 12h50 [96]. Turkish served 14 US airports with about 21 daily departures in Q3 2026, trimmed JFK from 28 to 25 weekly for summer 2026, and has announced Minneapolis and Philadelphia [97][98]. Fares: unsourced.
- **Airport to clinics:** Istanbul Airport (IST) is about 42 km from Taksim/Şişli; 35–90 minutes depending on traffic; Havaist bus and M11 metro exist; packages almost always include a private transfer [99].
- **Hotels:** Şişli 3-star listings ran about $47–$85 a night and Marriott Şişli about $138 in late 2025/early 2026 snapshots; cheapest in January [100].
- **Length of stay and flying home:** CDC: wait 10–14 days after major surgery before flying [84]. The Aesthetic Society advises against flying for about a month after significant cosmetic surgery [101]. Bariatric sources commonly say 4–6 weeks, with clot risk highest in the first two weeks; an NHS leaflet notes travel of 4+ hours doubles DVT risk and risk stays elevated for about four weeks [102][103]. Hair transplant packages are built around 3 nights with flights 2–7 days post-op [12][104]. Dental implants: 48–72 hours minimum, 1–2 weeks after bone grafting [105]. Typical Turkish packages send cosmetic patients home within days, which UK surgeons repeatedly flag as the structural risk [78].
- **Seasonality:** Istanbul highs about 29–30°C in July–August, lows near 3–6°C in January–February; summer is driest, December wettest [106]. 2026 holidays when clinics may close: Ramazan Bayramı 20–22 March; Kurban Bayramı 27–30 May (eve 26 May) [107]. Antalya demand is said to rise September–December [3].
- **Payment:** Large private chains invoice foreign patients in USD or EUR, insulating them from lira moves [108]. Clinics commonly take a deposit; the rest at the clinic by card, cash or bank transfer; refund terms are rarely published and should be obtained in writing [109].
- **What packages include:** procedure, 1–2 hospital nights where relevant, hotel, airport and clinic transfers, interpreter, basic meds and an aftercare kit (shampoo/lotion for hair; garments for body surgery), sometimes PRP sessions; airfare is almost always excluded; long-term follow-up usually is [109][110].
- **Follow-up in the US:** ASPS warns that complications from surgery abroad can be severe and expensive once home and that treating medical-tourism complications may cost the US system $1.3 billion a year [111][112]. Medicare does not cover care outside the US except narrow emergencies; most dental insurance excludes care abroad; specialist "complications" policies exist that pay only for post-procedure problems, not the procedure [113][114][115]. Dentists note missing implant brand and lot documentation makes later management harder, and some report concern about malpractice coverage when treating others' work [116].

## 6. Red flags and how to vet

Red flags, each tied to a documented harm:
- No MoH authorization certificate, or a facility not on the MoH list [50][52].
- Hair transplant done outside a hospital, medical center or polyclinic (hotel room, office, "studio") [59][63].
- No named surgeon, no surgeon consultation before the day of surgery, or the surgeon not present for the whole operation [61][73].
- Promised graft counts above roughly 4,000–4,500 in one session [16][8].
- Multiple major procedures bundled into one trip (BBL + tummy tuck + liposuction) and discharge within days [73][78].
- Sold as a "holiday," "VIP package" or "summer sale" (ASA found this trivializes surgery) [87][88].
- WhatsApp-only sales through an intermediary that is not USHAŞ-certified [45][47].
- Guaranteed results or "lifetime warranty" with no written terms and no insurer behind them [46].
- Prices far below the median ranges in section 2.
- "Veneers" quoted without a per-tooth plan showing whether teeth will be crowned [25][23].
- Pressure to proceed despite self-declared health history and no independent pre-op checks (a UK coroner criticized a referral firm that relied on self-declaration) [117].

Questions to ask and get in writing: certificate numbers (facility and agency); surgeon's name, specialty, MoH registration; who performs each step; hospital where surgery is done and its emergency capacity; complication insurance policy details (now legally required) and how a foreign patient claims; exact inclusions, deposit and refund terms, revision policy; implant brand and lot numbers for dental and breast work; discharge criteria and minimum stay before flying; who to contact after return and in what language; full medical records and operative notes before leaving Turkey (CDC checklist) [84].

## 8. Frequently asked questions (sourced)

1. **Is medical tourism to Turkey safe?** Outcomes are uneven. Turkey has JCI-accredited hospital groups [53], but 61–78% of UK patients treated for complications after surgery abroad had it done in Turkey, and 7 British nationals died there after procedures in 2025 [51][78][80].
2. **Why is it so cheap?** Lower labor and facility costs, lira depreciation, package pricing, and in hair transplants the use of technicians for most of the procedure [63][108].
3. **Do Americans need a visa?** No, for stays up to 90 days in 180 [90].
4. **How long is the flight?** About 10 hours from New York, 10.5 from Chicago, 13 from Los Angeles [93][94][95].
5. **How do I check a clinic is licensed?** Find it on the MoH authorized facility list or HealthTürkiye portal and ask for the certificate number [50][52].
6. **What does JCI accreditation mean?** Hospital-level accreditation of processes; about 38 Turkish hospitals hold it; CDC notes accreditation does not guarantee outcome [53][84].
7. **Who actually does my hair transplant?** By regulation, a physician opens the channels; certified technicians may harvest and implant under supervision [61].
8. **How many grafts is realistic?** ISHRS members average about 2,350 grafts in a first session; 4,500 in one session is a red flag [15][16].
9. **How many days for a hair transplant trip?** Packages are built on 3 nights; clinics advise 2–7 days before flying [12][104].
10. **When can I fly after surgery?** CDC: 10–14 days after major surgery; aesthetic surgeons: about a month; bariatric sources: 4–6 weeks [84][101][102].
11. **What is "Turkey teeth"?** Slang for aggressive full crowns on filed-down healthy teeth sold as "veneers"; UK dentists report crowns and implants as the top remedial cases [23][25].
12. **How much do veneers cost?** About $145–$400 per tooth in Turkey vs $900–$2,500 in the US (marketing figures) [17][18].
13. **How much is a gastric sleeve?** About $3,200–$7,500 all-inclusive in Turkey vs about $14,900 average US self-pay [30][31][33].
14. **Can I get donor eggs or a surrogate in Turkey?** No; only married heterosexual couples using their own gametes; donation and surrogacy are illegal [36][37][38].
15. **Will my US insurance cover complications?** Generally no; Medicare excludes foreign care outside narrow emergencies, and most dental plans exclude care abroad [113][114].
16. **Can I sue if something goes wrong?** Possible in Turkish courts, in Turkish, usually via power of attorney; practically difficult, and several UK inquests could not trace the surgeon [86][71].
17. **Is complication insurance required?** Yes for operating-room procedures under the April 2025 regulation; how foreigners claim is not yet documented [46][47].
18. **Do clinics take cards? What currency?** Private chains invoice in USD or EUR; deposits by transfer and balance by card or cash are common; get terms in writing [108][109].
19. **Where are the clinics in Istanbul?** Mostly European side: Şişli, Nişantaşı, Fulya, Mecidiyeköy, Levent; big hospital campuses also in Maslak, Ataşehir and Bağcılar [9].
20. **Is Antalya a good alternative?** It is the second-largest health-tourism market, dental-heavy, with about 90% foreign patients in dental clinics [3].
21. **Has the US government warned about it?** No Turkey-specific warning; State Dept Level 2 for security reasons; the UK FCDO has a dedicated warning [92][51].
22. **Will a US surgeon or dentist fix problems?** Emergencies yes; elective revisions at full cost; clinicians cite missing records and liability concerns [111][116].
23. **What should I bring home?** All records, imaging, implant brand and lot numbers, operative notes, and medication lists, translated (CDC) [84].

## 9. Trends 2025–2026

- **Volumes down, revenue flat.** 2025 health visitors fell about 7% to 1.40 million with revenue flat at about $3.02 billion; H1 2026 visitors were again below the prior year while revenue per patient rose to about $2,600 [2][3].
- **Lira and inflation.** Official CPI was about 31% in 2025 (56% per the independent ENAG index) [125]; USD/TRY was about 48.6 in September 2026, down about 17.5% over 12 months [126]. Chains bill in hard currency, so dollar prices have not fallen with the lira; a Medicana executive warned that a stable lira would erode Turkey's price advantage [4][108].
- **Regulation.** April 2025 regulation (complication insurance, TÜSKA accreditation by end-2026, HealthTürkiye portal, intermediary oversight) [46][47][49]; September 2025 hair transplant amendment extended compliance to end-2026 and reset inspections [62]; October 2025 debate over technicians' role [61].
- **UK positions.** FCDO: 7 deaths in 2025 [51]. BAAPS 2026 database paper and BMJ Open review quantified NHS costs [77][80]. ASA banned "holiday" framing in ads in 2023 [87]. NHS treats emergencies but not routine elective correction [78].
- **Flights.** Turkish Airlines trimmed some US frequencies for summer 2026 while announcing Minneapolis and Philadelphia; Denver and Detroit were earlier additions [97][98][96].
- **Antalya.** Positioned as the second hub, dental-led, with provincial promotion around COP31 [3].
- **High-profile cases.** Latchman (July 2025) and Rama (Nov 2025) hair-transplant deaths in Istanbul [74][76]; Canadian patient death in Antalya (March 2026) [68]; a Brandeis brief (April 2026) argued state promotion of medical tourism widens domestic access inequalities [127].
